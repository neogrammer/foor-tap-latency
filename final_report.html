Abstract. Latency thresholds are well established for hand input and for whole-body interaction in virtual reality, but not for the foot, despite the foot being used increasingly as a secondary input channel. This project built a measurement instrument for that question: a Meta Quest 3 application in which a participant taps a floor-mounted switch in time with a metronome while the system inserts a controlled delay between the tap and its confirmation. Five delays were intended (0, 50, 100, 200, 400 ms) and two measures were taken per block, the spread of tap-to-beat asynchrony and a self-reported responsiveness rating, plus NASA-TLX and a preference ranking at the end of each session. Three participants completed full sessions, 887 scored taps in total: two on a hand controller laid on the floor, and a third on the foot pedal that the final release introduces. The first result concerns the instrument rather than the participants: once the system was made to report the latency it actually delivered rather than the latency it requested, the 0 ms and 50 ms conditions were both found to deliver approximately 63 ms, the floor imposed by the audio output buffer, and the pedal session reproduced that floor at 62 ms on different input hardware. The design therefore resolves four distinct levels, not five, and the region below the floor is not addressable by this apparatus. Within those four levels, two of the three participants rated responsiveness sharply lower at 200 ms and above, one of them with timing precision degrading in step, while the third showed no sensitivity to the manipulation; no degradation threshold could be fitted with confidence at this sample size. Returning to the session logs for the final release exposed two further properties of the original apparatus: the display rate was not the 72 Hz that had been assumed but whatever the headset chose on the day, and the controller's tap timestamp was locked to the display frame rather than marking the moment of the press. The final release therefore replaces the floor controller with a foot pedal that is timestamped at the switch and synchronised to the headset's clock, and that recovered press times to within about 2 ms in bench tests across a deliberately degraded network. The contribution is the instrument, its characterisation, and a method for reporting latency honestly in the presence of an uncalibrated pipeline.

1. Introduction

End-to-end latency is one of the oldest known determinants of interactive performance. MacKenzie and Ware demonstrated in 1993 that lag degrades pointing throughput in a way that behaves like reduced target size, and the effect has been replicated across input devices and display technologies since. In virtual reality the question has been pursued aggressively, because the consequences extend beyond throughput to presence and to simulator sickness. Friston and colleagues placed the lower bound on noticeable latency for hand-based pointing and steering near 16 ms; Waltemate and colleagues reported a threshold near 75 ms for closed-loop whole-body interaction.

The foot has been studied as a virtual reality input channel, but largely in terms of what it can accomplish rather than how fast it must be served. Work on foot-based text entry and on foot interaction for secondary tasks establishes that the foot is viable as an input channel while the hands are occupied. What is missing is the figure a designer would actually need: how much delay can be inserted between a foot action and its system response before timing performance degrades, and before the user notices. Hand and whole-body numbers are not obviously transferable. The foot is slower and coarser than the hand but is also used differently, and the motor loop that controls it is not the same one.

This project asked three questions:

  • RQ1. At what added latency does foot timing performance measurably degrade?
  • RQ2. At what added latency do users report noticing the lag?
  • RQ3. What is the gap between those two points?

RQ3 is the question with design consequences. If performance degrades well before anyone notices, a system can be unresponsive in ways its users cannot articulate. If the reverse holds, complaints arrive before any measurable cost.

The work reported here is a pilot conducted as a course project. It does not produce a threshold, and a substantial part of what it did produce is a finding about the measuring instrument rather than about feet. That outcome is reported in full, because the instrument's floor is precisely the kind of limitation that is easy to leave unstated and that would invalidate any threshold claimed on top of it.

2. Related work

Latency and performance

MacKenzie and Ware [4] established the canonical result that lag degrades pointing performance systematically and that its effect is multiplicative with task difficulty. Their experimental logic, varying a controlled delay across blocks and measuring a performance index per block, is the structure this study follows.

Friston, Karlström and Steed [2] examined very low latencies in virtual reality and found effects on pointing and steering down to approximately 16 ms, which is below the frame interval of most consumer headsets. Waltemate and colleagues [6] studied closed-loop whole-body interaction and reported both perceptual and motor effects, with a threshold around 75 ms. Those two figures bracket the range this study intended to probe. As Section 5 reports, the apparatus built here cannot resolve most of that range.

Sensorimotor synchronisation

The task used here, tapping in time with an isochronous auditory metronome, is one of the most studied paradigms in motor timing. Repp's review [5] documents two properties this study depends on. First, people tap slightly before the beat rather than on it, a robust effect termed negative mean asynchrony, typically between 20 and 80 ms and varying considerably between individuals and with musical training. Second, the standard deviation of asynchrony is the conventional measure of timing precision and is more informative than mean error, because it is insensitive to a constant offset. That second property is what makes the measure usable here despite an uncalibrated pipeline.

Timing validity in experiment software

Bridges, Pitiot, MacAskill and Peirce [1] measured the timing accuracy of a range of experiment generators and reached a conclusion that shaped this project's engineering: platform-reported timing figures are best-case, and every setup should be validated against external hardware rather than trusted. Their finding is the direct reason this report treats its own latency figures as provisional and devotes Section 5.1 to what the apparatus actually delivers rather than what it requests.

Workload measurement

NASA-TLX [3] is used here in a reduced form to capture subjective workload at the end of each session. The deviations from the published instrument are stated in Section 4.4.

3. System

The system is two programs with a data file between them. A native application on the Meta Quest 3 runs the experiment and writes an event log; a self-contained browser page reads that log and performs all analysis. No statistic is computed on the headset. This separation was deliberate: a mistake in the pairing rule or in a derived measure is then a change to an HTML file rather than a reason to recall a participant.

3.1 Apparatus

The headset application is written in C++ against OpenXR with a Vulkan renderer, built on the Khronos OpenXR tutorial scaffold. It accepts a tap from either of two inputs. Two of the three sessions reported in Section 5 used a hand controller lying flat on the floor and tapped with the foot; the tap action is bound to most buttons on both controllers, because a foot cannot aim, and the floor unit is distinguished from any hand-held unit at the point the action is read rather than in the binding. The final release adds a foot pedal as the primary input, used for the third session, and keeps the controller as a fallback. The pedal is a momentary foot switch wired to an input pin of a Raspberry Pi 5, which timestamps each press and sends it to the headset over the local wireless network. Its design is set out in Section 3.4, after the timing architecture it has to fit into, and the reasons for it are a result of this project (Section 5.7).

Because no controller is held in the hand, the interface is driven by hand tracking. Pointing a finger moves a cursor on a floating panel and holding it on a target for one second activates it. An earlier pinch-based selection was abandoned: the aim ray runs through the index finger, pinching curls that finger, and the ray therefore swings away at the moment of selection. Dwell removes the cause rather than compensating for it, and the finger never leaves the pointing pose. Hand tracking is used for menus only and never for anything timed, since it is camera-based and slower than a controller.

3.2 Timing architecture

The entire study reduces to one subtraction: the time a foot landed minus the time its beat occurred. A display frame lasts 11.1 or 13.9 ms, depending on a refresh rate the headset chooses (Section 5.7), and the smallest intended delay was 50 ms. Working at frame resolution would have spent a fifth or more of that condition on rounding before the participant moved. Three choices were made to keep both ends of the subtraction finer than a frame:

  1. The metronome click is scheduled at an exact audio sample. The Android AAudio interface returns a matched pair of audio frame position and system clock time, so the arrival time of a future sample is known before it occurs. Each beat is logged at the time it will be heard, not at the frame on which something was drawn.
  2. The tap time is read from XrActionStateBoolean::lastChangeTime, on the understanding that this is the runtime's own record of when the button state changed rather than the frame on which the change was noticed. Section 5.7 shows that this understanding does not hold on this runtime, and Section 3.4 describes the input that replaces it.
  3. The confirmation click is scheduled by the same sample-accurate mechanism as the metronome, and the time at which it was actually scheduled is logged.

That third point is the one that produced the principal finding and is developed in Section 5.1.

3.3 Analysis tool

The analysis page pairs each tap with at most one beat. Every candidate pair within half an inter-onset interval is scored by absolute distance and consumed closest-first, so no beat can be credited to two taps and unmatched taps are reported rather than absorbed. It derives the delivered latency per block from the logged confirmation events, fits a flat-then-turning curve to each measure, and plots participants as separate series. The threshold fit reports a breakpoint only when its residual error is at least twice as good as the next candidate; otherwise it reports that no breakpoint is separable. The tool is linked at the top of this report and runs on the session files in the repository.

3.4 Pedal input and clock synchronisation

The pedal exists to put a trustworthy time on the press. A packet's arrival at the headset is not that time: the wireless hop adds several milliseconds, and a different number each time. The design therefore reads the time at the switch and carries it in the packet.

  • The press is stamped at the pin. The relay on the Pi uses the Linux kernel's own timestamp for the edge, so the interval before user code is told about it (measured at 0.6 to 0.7 ms on this Pi) is excluded from the measurement.
  • Debounce does not move the time. A press is accepted once the contact has held for 3 ms, which rejects electrical spikes on a two-metre cable, but the time reported is that of the first edge. A release must hold for 15 ms, so a contact chattering under a resting foot is not counted as a second tap.
  • The two clocks are related by measurement. Twenty times a second the headset sends a probe carrying its own send time, and the Pi replies with the time the probe arrived and the time the reply left. The four timestamps give the offset between the clocks and the round-trip time. The offset from the fastest round trip in the last three seconds is used, since a fast exchange has the least room for asymmetry, and half of that round trip bounds the error of the result.
  • The same probes find the pedal. They are broadcast until a relay answers, so no network address is configured on either device and the apparatus can move to another network unchanged. They also keep the headset's radio from entering power save between taps, which would otherwise hold a packet until its next wake-up.
  • A lost packet does not lose a tap. Each press is sent twice, 3 ms apart, and the most recent press is repeated inside every later packet. Sequence numbers remove the duplicates and reveal any press that never arrived.
  • One time base. The pedal press, the metronome beat and the confirmation click all pass through the same conversion from the system clock to the runtime clock. Whatever constant error that conversion carries (Section 5.7) is then common to all three and cancels in both the asynchrony and the delivered latency. The conversion is also held fixed for the duration of each block.
  • Nothing else answers the pedal. During a block the application gives no visual or auditory response to a press other than the delayed confirmation. An immediate indicator would supply exactly the zero-latency feedback the study withholds.

The session log records which input produced the taps, how many pedal taps lacked a source timestamp, the median time between a press and the frame that acted on it, the median round trip behind the clock offset, any presses lost or reconstructed, and the frame period actually observed. The intent is the one Section 5.1 arrives at: the instrument reports what it did.

4. Method

4.1 Task

The participant stands in a minimal virtual room. An auditory metronome sounds at a fixed interval of 600 ms, accompanied by a visual pulse. The participant taps the floor switch with one foot in time with the metronome. After each tap the system waits a predetermined interval and then confirms the tap with an audible click and a flash of the floor pad. The metronome itself is never delayed.

4.2 Design

A within-subjects design with five intended delay conditions: 0, 50, 100, 200 and 400 ms. These were chosen to bracket the published figures, spanning Friston's 16 ms lower bound and Waltemate's 75 ms whole-body threshold. Each block comprises four unscored lead-in beats followed by 60 scored taps. A practice block at zero delay precedes the scored blocks and is not recorded.

Condition order was counterbalanced by rotating a cyclic square keyed to the participant number, so each participant received a different order (Table 1). This balances serial position but not first-order carryover; a Williams design is the correct structure and is identified as future work.

4.3 Measures

  • Asynchrony. The signed interval between each tap and its paired beat, negative when the tap precedes the beat.
  • Precision. The sample standard deviation of asynchrony within a block. This is the primary performance measure for RQ1, and it is insensitive to any constant offset in the pipeline.
  • Perceived responsiveness. A 1 to 7 rating collected after every block, anchored LAGGY at 1 and INSTANT at 7. This is the measure for RQ2.
  • Workload. NASA-TLX at the end of the session.
  • Preference. A forced ranking of the five blocks from most to least responsive, collected after the workload questions.

Hit rate within a fixed window was collected during earlier milestones and has been withdrawn. With mean asynchrony near 100 ms and a window of ±100 ms, almost no tap counted as a hit regardless of how steadily a participant tapped: the measure was describing the pipeline's constant offset rather than the participant's performance. It can be restored once the offset is measured, by centring the window on it.

4.4 Deviations from published instruments

NASA-TLX is administered here as six unweighted items on a 1 to 7 scale rather than the published 21-point weighted instrument. The constraint is the renderer: text in this application is drawn by composing letterforms from rotated cuboids, and a 21-point response row does not fit beside its anchor labels. The reduced form supports comparison between participants but does not yield a standard TLX score. The Performance item retains the original good-to-poor direction and is therefore reverse-scored relative to the other five.

4.5 Participants and procedure

Three adults completed full sessions. This is a course project rather than human subjects research, and the instructor confirmed that two participants was the expected scale; no IRB review was required or sought. P01 and P02 were run on 18 October 2026 with the floor controller. P03 was run on 13 November 2026 with the foot pedal. None of the three was told what was being manipulated. The instruction given was to tap in time with the beat as accurately as possible and to answer the questions between blocks. Measured from the start of the first scored block to the final ranking, the sessions lasted 5.3, 10.9 and 5.5 minutes, preceded in each case by an unrecorded practice block.

All three sessions ran with audio sample-accurate scheduling active (audio_timed=1) and with the clock-conversion extension unavailable (clock_exact=0); the consequence is discussed in Section 6.3. The two controller sessions ran at a display rate of 90 Hz and the pedal session at 72 Hz. The application requests no rate, and the difference was found only in the logs (Section 5.7).

5. Results

5.1 The apparatus has a floor of approximately 63 ms

The first result concerns the instrument. During pilot testing a participant reported that two of the five blocks produced no confirmation sound at all, and articulated why it mattered more clearly than the design had: without the confirmation there is nothing against which to judge the delay, so the baseline condition ceases to function as a baseline.

The cause was in the click scheduler. A confirmation is scheduled for the moment of the tap plus the condition delay. At 0 ms that moment has already passed by the time the audio thread reads the queue, and the scheduler discarded any click whose target frame lay behind the write head, which is correct behaviour for a metronome scheduled well in advance and silently wrong for tap feedback. The scheduler was changed to clamp a late click forward to the earliest frame the stream can produce, to return the frame it actually used, and to log the real delivery time.

That change converted a silent failure into a measurement. Across both controller sessions the 0 ms and 50 ms conditions both delivered approximately 63 ms (Table 1): 63.0 and 63.3 ms for P01, 62.9 and 63.0 ms for P02. This is the floor imposed by the audio output buffer, and it was present throughout the project while being invisible to it. Section 5.7 refines what the figure of 63 ms corresponds to physically; the equivalence of the two conditions does not depend on that refinement.

The pedal session reproduces the finding on different input hardware and in a different time base. P03's 0 ms and 50 ms conditions delivered medians of 62.4 and 61.8 ms, and no confirmation in the 50 ms block arrived sooner than 53 ms. On the pedal the floor is no longer a single value. A press has to cross a wireless link before its confirmation can be scheduled, so the delivered latency in those two blocks ranged from 53 to 82 ms from tap to tap, with one press held to 136 ms by a stalled packet, where the controller sessions logged the same figure every time. Above the floor the pedal session delivered exactly 100, 200 and 400 ms on every tap.

Two consequences follow. First, the design resolves four distinct levels rather than five, since the two shortest conditions are the same stimulus. Second, and more seriously for the original question, the range below 63 ms is not addressable by this apparatus at all, and that range contains Friston's 16 ms bound and most of the interval up to Waltemate's 75 ms threshold. The study as designed could not have answered its own question, and the only reason this is known is that the instrument was made to report what it delivered instead of what it was asked for.

5.2 Timing precision

Grouping by delivered latency, and pooling the two blocks that landed at the floor, gives four levels per participant (Figure 1).

P01's precision degrades monotonically with delivered latency, from 37.1 ms at the floor through 41.2, 62.4 and 76.1 ms. P02 shows no such ordering. Their worst block, 108.9 ms, occurred at 100 ms of delay, which was also the first scored block of their session; a practice effect is the most parsimonious explanation and is indistinguishable from a condition effect with one participant.

P03's precision is worse at 200 and 400 ms (63.8 and 46.9 ms) than at the three shorter conditions (34.5, 31.4 and 23.0 ms), which taken alone would read as an effect of delay. It cannot be read that way. P03's order placed 200 and 400 ms first, and their precision improved with every successive block of the session, so delay and practice are completely confounded. Two of the three participants produced their least precise block first.

5.3 Perceived responsiveness

P01's ratings fall monotonically and traverse almost the entire scale, from 6 at the floor to 1 at 400 ms. P02's ratings are effectively flat, ranging only between 5 and 7, and the 200 ms block received the highest rating of their session.

P03's ratings divide the conditions into two groups: 6, 7 and 7 for the three shortest, and 3 and 2 for 200 and 400 ms. Order cannot be excluded here either, since P03's two lowest ratings went to the first two blocks they experienced. But the pattern matches P01's, whose long delays came third and fourth and whose rating recovered to 6 in the final block, and in both participants the drop falls between 100 and 200 ms.

5.4 No threshold is separable

The analysis fits a two-segment model to each curve, flat up to a breakpoint and linear thereafter, searching over the condition values and rejecting any candidate whose second segment slopes the wrong way. It reports a breakpoint only when its residual error is at least twice as good as the next candidate.

For all three participants, on both measures, no breakpoint meets that bar. The best fits and their margins are: P01 precision at 100 ms (margin 1.07), P01 rating at 50 ms (margin 1.96), P02 precision at 50 ms (margin 1.66), P02 rating at 200 ms (margin 1.17), P03 precision at 50 ms (margin 1.09), P03 rating at 50 ms (margin 1.03). A margin near 1 means the next candidate fits essentially as well.

This is the correct outcome rather than a disappointing one. With four levels, three participants on two different inputs, and an uncalibrated floor, any confidently stated threshold would be an artefact of the fitting procedure. RQ1, RQ2 and RQ3 are therefore not answered by this data. What the data does support is a direction. For P01 both measures move consistently across the range, and at 400 ms the degradation is unambiguous on both. P03's ratings point the same way. In both, perceived responsiveness is intact at 100 ms and clearly reduced at 200 ms, which marks the interval between those two values as the one a follow-up study should sample most finely.

5.5 Preference ranking

After the workload questions each participant ranked the five blocks from most to least responsive, choosing from block numbers without ever seeing a latency value.

  • P01 ranked them 50 ms, 0 ms, 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms. Given that the two shortest conditions are the same stimulus, this ordering is correct.
  • P02 ranked them 0 ms, 400 ms, 100 ms, 50 ms, 200 ms, which bears no relation to the delivered latencies.
  • P03 ranked them 100 ms, 50 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms, 0 ms. The three delays above the floor are in the correct order. The two floor-level blocks, which are one stimulus, were placed second and last.

P01's ranking is the single cleanest result in the study. It is a five-way forced ordering produced from memory at the end of the session, and it recovers the experimental manipulation exactly. P02's does not differ from chance. P03's is correct wherever the stimuli differed and inconsistent only where they did not.

The rankings carry a caveat that the other measures do not. At this point in the session the screen read PICK NUMBER 1 above a row of block numbers, with the direction of the ranking stated only on the screen before. P03 stopped to ask whether the first pick was meant to be the most responsive block or the least. The order P03 then gave is consistent with the intended direction, but the question shows the prompt could be read either way, and P02, whose ranking looks arbitrary, was given the same prompt and did not ask. P02's ranking may therefore reflect the wording as much as the participant. The release build names what is being picked on every screen of the ranking, in the same words the rating scale uses.

5.6 Workload

The workload profiles differ in a way that is consistent with the performance data. P02 reported the scale maximum on physical demand (7) and effort (7) against P01's 3 and 4, while simultaneously giving the most favourable available response on the performance item. The coherent reading is that P02 was working hard at the tapping task itself and had no attention left for the property being manipulated. P03's profile lies between the two, with effort at 6 and physical demand at 4. P01 and P02 rated temporal demand at 6 and P03 at 5, which is expected for a task paced by an external metronome.

5.7 Two properties of the original apparatus, found in its own logs

Preparing the final release meant asking of the session logs two questions they had not been collected to answer. Both answers correct statements made earlier in this project.

The display rate is chosen by the headset, and it changed. Every logged event carries the predicted display time of the frame that processed it. Across the two controller sessions the 1,347 intervals between consecutive such times are whole multiples of 11.11 ms, with a median residual of 2 microseconds; tested against the 13.89 ms period of a 72 Hz display the median residual is 2.8 ms. Those sessions ran at 90 Hz, not the 72 Hz stated in earlier milestones. The pedal session, four weeks later on the same headset, did run at 72 Hz: its intervals are whole multiples of 13.89 ms to within a microsecond. The application requests no rate, so the figure given in earlier milestones was an assumption that happened to be false for the sessions it described and true for one not yet run. The release build writes the observed frame period into each session's header, so that it is read from the session and not assumed.

The controller's tap timestamp is locked to the frame. If lastChangeTime recorded the moment a button went down, the interval between it and the display time of the frame that noticed the change would vary over at least one frame period, because a press can fall anywhere between two samplings of the controller. It does not. Across all 622 logged taps that interval lies between 19.4 and 22.3 ms, a range of 3.0 ms where at least 11.1 ms was required (Figure 4). On this runtime the value is the time at which the frame sampled the controller. It is not the time of the press.

Three consequences follow for the controller data in this section, and none of them alters a conclusion drawn from it.

  • Each controller tap is recorded late by between zero and one frame, plus the controller's radio latency, which is unmeasured. A uniform error over 11.1 ms has a standard deviation of 3.2 ms. Removed in quadrature from the block standard deviations of P01 and P02 in Table 1, which range from 33.9 to 108.9 ms, it changes none of them by more than 0.2 ms.
  • The same interval measures a constant that could previously only be estimated. The clock conversion equates the present moment on the audio clock with the predicted display time of the current frame, while the controller's timestamps sit a median of 21 ms before it. Every time derived from the audio clock, each beat and each confirmation, is therefore logged 21 ms late relative to the controller taps.
  • For the figures in Table 1 that constant has a definite meaning. A confirmation logged as delivered at L ms was heard L − 21 ms after the frame that sampled the tap, and so about L − 15 ms plus the radio latency after the press itself, taking the mean sampling delay as half a frame. The floor logged as 63 ms is on the same reckoning about 48 ms plus radio latency after the press. Mean asynchrony is logged about 15 ms more negative than it was, less the radio latency.

These are constant shifts. They leave the spacing between conditions, the standard deviations and every comparison in Sections 5.2 to 5.6 as reported, and they move the floor without removing it: the two shortest conditions remain a single stimulus. Their importance is for the instrument. Neither property was visible in any per-block summary. Both were found by the method of Section 5.1 applied a second time, asking what the system did instead of what it was believed to do, and together they are the reason the final release moves the tap onto an input whose time is read at its source.

6. Discussion

6.1 The instrument is the result

This study set out to locate a latency threshold for foot input and did not find one. What it produced instead is a characterised instrument and a measurement of its own floor. That is a smaller claim, but it is a real one, and it is a prerequisite for the original question rather than a substitute for it. A threshold reported without knowing that the two shortest conditions were identical would have been wrong in a way nobody could have detected from the output.

The mechanism by which the floor was found is worth stating plainly, because it generalises. The system was asked to report what it actually did rather than what it was instructed to do, and the two turned out to differ. Bridges and colleagues [1] make the same argument about experiment software generally. Any latency study that assumes its requested delays are its delivered delays is making an unverified assumption, and in this case that assumption was false for 40% of the conditions.

6.2 Individual differences are larger than expected

The participants differ more than the conditions do. P01 produces a textbook dose-response on both measures and a perfect preference ranking; P02 produces neither; P03 reproduces P01's ratings but not P01's precision curve. With n = 3 this cannot be decomposed into a trait difference, a strategy difference, or a practice difference, but its size is itself informative for designing the next round: a study that assumes homogeneous latency sensitivity in this task would be poorly powered.

The mean asynchrony figures point the same way. P01 tapped on average 109.7 ms before the beat and P02 52.0 ms, a difference of 57.7 ms between two people on identical hardware. A constant pipeline offset cannot produce a between-participant difference, so at least that much of the asynchrony is the person rather than the instrument. This materially revises an earlier reading of this project's data. At the alpha milestone, with 24 taps per block and a malfunctioning rating control, mean asynchrony was approximately 175 ms and was interpreted as a large unexplained instrument offset. With 60 taps per block and the audio path corrected, P02's figure of 52.0 ms sits inside the 20 to 80 ms range Repp [5] reports as normal, and P01's 109.7 ms exceeds it only modestly. The apparatus is in considerably better condition than the earlier estimate implied.

P03's mean asynchrony of 110.6 ms was measured on the pedal, in a time base that does not carry the constants of Section 5.7, and it is not compared with the two controller figures. It does show that a lead of this size is not produced by the controller path alone. Whether it reflects the participant, the foot, or the point in the pedal's travel at which the switch closes, which is before the foot reaches the end of its stroke, cannot be determined from one session.

6.3 What remains uncalibrated

One constant has now been measured and one has not. The clock-conversion extension XR_KHR_convert_timespec_time does not load on this headset, so the audio clock and the runtime clock are related by an estimate derived from predicted display time, recorded in the session files as clock_exact=0. At the beta milestone the resulting error was described as probably 15 to 30 ms. Section 5.7 puts it at 21 ms relative to the controller's timestamps, from the logs themselves.

What remains unmeasured is the physical path at both ends: the radio latency between a press on the controller and the frame that samples it, and the interval between the presentation time the audio stream reports and sound reaching the ear. No software on the headset can observe either. Both affect absolute figures and neither affects comparisons between conditions, since a constant offset shifts every condition equally and the primary measure is a standard deviation. The claims in Sections 5.2 and 5.3 are therefore not threatened by these gaps, but any attempt to compare absolute asynchrony to published values is.

6.4 The final release: a pedal timed at its source

The apparatus delivered with this report differs in its input from the one that collected the first two sessions. A first pedal prototype, built after data collection, relayed each press over the network and let the headset stamp it on arrival. That design was not admitted to the timing path, because it would have placed the Pi's input handling, the network stack and a wireless hop inside the measured interval. The release version is the design of Section 3.4, in which the time is read at the switch and the network carries it without being able to alter it.

Because the purpose of the change is a claim about timing, it was tested as one. The relay and the headset's receiver were run unmodified on a single machine with a simulated wireless link between them and the relay's clock set wrong by sixteen minutes. Since both programs then share one physical clock, the true time of every simulated press is known exactly, and the error of the time the receiver recovers can be read off directly (Table 3).

On a quiet link the recovered press time was within 0.1 ms of the truth for every press. On a link with 79 dropped packets, random delay and stalls of up to 60 ms, every press was still delivered and the worst error was 2.1 ms, against arrival delays of up to 48 ms; the median error of 0.9 ms is half the 2 ms asymmetry built into that link, which is the error a symmetric-path assumption predicts. With every press packet destroyed in transit, all 20 presses were reconstructed from later packets with the correct time. The same harness confirms that a restart of the relay, a headset started after the pedal has been in use, an electrical spike, contact bounce and a dropped kernel edge each behave as intended. A further test replays a whole simulated session through the experiment logic of both builds with the controller as input: the release build logs events identical, row for row, to the build that collected P01 and P02, so the change of input has not disturbed the path that data came from. The tests are in the repository.

On the hardware itself, the headset located the relay without configuration in under a second and held a round trip of 2.6 ms on a home network, with 190 of 191 probes answered in ten seconds. That bounds the clock error at about 1.3 ms. The third session in Section 5 was collected this way, and its header describes the link over a whole session: 318 tap events, none without a source timestamp, none lost and none reconstructed; a median of 15 ms between a press and the frame that acted on it, with one press delayed to 88 ms by a stalled packet; and a median round trip of 2.6 ms behind the clock offset.

The limits of this should be stated as plainly as its results. One participant has been run on the pedal and two on the controller; Section 5 marks which is which, and absolute asynchrony is not compared across the two. The pedal does not lower the audio floor, and the 3 ms spent confirming a press, together with the wireless hop, delays how soon a confirmation can follow one, so the two shortest conditions remain merged. A metal foot switch also makes a mechanical click of its own at the moment of contact, an undelayed sound that a silent controller button does not make. P03 heard it on every tap, and a study run on the pedal would need to mask it or account for it.

7. Limitations

  • The floor. The pipeline cannot deliver a response faster than approximately 63 ms, so two of five conditions are one condition and the region of greatest theoretical interest is unreachable.
  • Sample size. Three participants, who do not agree. Nothing here approaches statistical significance and none of the three research questions is answered.
  • Two inputs. P01 and P02 used the floor controller and P03 the foot pedal. Precision and ratings are compared across them; absolute asynchrony is not, for the reasons in Section 5.7.
  • Counterbalancing and practice. The rotating order balances serial position but not first-order carryover. P02 and P03 both produced their least precise block first, and P03's precision improved with every block, so a practice effect cannot be separated from a condition effect. The practice phase is too short.
  • Display rate. The headset ran two sessions at 90 Hz and one at 72 Hz. The release build records the rate but does not yet fix it.
  • Ranking prompt. All three rankings were collected with a prompt that one participant could not interpret without asking (Section 5.5). It has been reworded for the release, but the rankings reported here predate the change, and P02's in particular should not be read as evidence of insensitivity.
  • Tap timestamps in the reported data. The controller's tap time is quantised to the 11.1 ms frame and carries an unmeasured radio latency (Section 5.7). The effect on the reported standard deviations is below 0.2 ms; the effect on absolute asynchrony is a constant.
  • Uncalibrated absolute timing. As Section 6.3 sets out, absolute asynchrony cannot be compared to published figures.
  • The pedal's own click. A metal foot switch clicks at the moment of contact. That is an undelayed sound the design does not yet mask, and it may have influenced P03's ratings.
  • Reduced workload instrument. The TLX variant used is not a standard score.
  • Audio routing. Sessions must run on the headset's own speakers; a Bluetooth audio device would add 100 ms or more, exceeding three of the four resolvable levels, and would do so silently.
  • Metronome regularity. The visual pulse lands on the next available frame, so the beat a participant sees is irregular by up to 11 ms. The logged beat time remains exact, so the measurement is unaffected, but the stimulus is imperfect.

8. Conclusion and future work

This project built a working instrument for measuring latency tolerance in foot-based virtual reality interaction, ran three full sessions through it, and established that the instrument has a response floor of approximately 63 ms that was invisible until the system was made to report its own behaviour, and that a second input path then reproduced at 62 ms. Within the four levels the apparatus can resolve, one participant degraded monotonically on both timing precision and perceived responsiveness and recovered the experimental ordering exactly from memory; a second showed no sensitivity to the manipulation at all; and a third, run on the pedal, matched the first on perceived responsiveness, both placing the loss between 100 and 200 ms. No threshold can be stated. The same habit of reading the logs for what the system did then showed that the display rate had been misstated and that the tap timestamp followed the frame rather than the foot, and the final release answers the second finding with a pedal whose press time is read at the switch and verified to about 2 ms.

The next steps follow directly from the limitations, in order of what they unblock:

  1. Measure the floor externally. High-speed video at 240 frames per second gives roughly 4 ms of resolution, enough to bracket the interval from pedal contact to audible confirmation and to determine how much of the 63 ms is the audio buffer and how much lies elsewhere. Until this number exists, every other refinement rests on an assumption.
  2. Respace the conditions above the measured floor. Two of five blocks currently deliver the same stimulus. Replacing them with levels the apparatus can distinguish is a one-line change and immediately increases the resolution of the design by 25%.
  3. Repeat the study on the pedal, with more participants. The distance between participants is the strongest argument the data makes, and it is an argument for a larger sample. Collected on the pedal, that sample would also be free of the frame quantisation and the conversion constant identified in Section 5.7.
  4. Adopt a Williams design and lengthen practice, so that first-order carryover is balanced rather than only serial position, and so that the first scored block is no longer the one in which participants are still learning the task.
  5. Request a fixed display rate, so that sessions are comparable by construction and not by inspection of their logs.
  6. Deal with the pedal's own click. Either mask it, for instance by presenting the audio over wired earphones, or measure its level at the ear and report it as part of the stimulus.

The methodological point generalises beyond this study. An interactive system that reports the latency it requested, rather than the latency it delivered, cannot be used to measure latency. Making that difference visible cost one change to a scheduler and produced the firmest number in this report, and asking the same question of the tap timestamps produced the next.
